# Time Estimation and Scheduling for Learning

Use this reference whenever the learner asks how long learning will take, requests a schedule, gives a deadline, wants time broken down, or needs a roadmap that fits recurring availability. Time estimates are planning ranges, not promises of mastery.

## 1. Separate four kinds of time

Never report one undifferentiated duration. Distinguish:

| Quantity | Meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Effort time | Focused work required to perform the activity | 45 minutes of deliberate practice |
| Session time | Effort plus setup, transition, notes, and shutdown | A 60-minute study block |
| Cycle time | Sessions plus recovery, spacing, and review between them | Several sessions until the exit check |
| Calendar time | Elapsed time under actual availability, interruptions, weekends, and dependencies | Two weeks to complete the cycle |

Learning may require calendar spacing even when effort is small. A one-hour concept may need several days of retrieval and transfer before the evidence is credible.

## 2. Decompose time at the right levels

Break a roadmap into:

`horizon → phase → cycle → milestone → learning unit → session → activity`

A **session** should be a bounded block with one dominant purpose and a visible stopping condition. A useful session decomposition is:

- setup and retrieval warm-up;
- new input or worked example;
- deliberate practice;
- feedback/debugging;
- retrieval or transfer check;
- notes, artifact update, and next-action capture;
- shutdown and context-switch allowance.

Do not schedule every leaf as a separate appointment. Group leaves that share context and produce one evidence artifact. Split a unit when it exceeds the learner’s reliable focus block, has multiple independent outcomes, or cannot produce a meaningful checkpoint.

## 3. Estimate in ranges, not false precision

Use at least a low, typical, and high estimate for uncertain work. Label the basis:

- **Analogous:** compared with a completed similar unit;
- **Bottom-up:** sum of activity estimates;
- **Observed-rate:** derived from the learner’s measured throughput;
- **Three-point:** low/most-likely/high estimate;
- **Dependency-based:** limited by prerequisite or review timing.

A simple three-point expected effort is `(low + 4 × typical + high) / 6`. Keep the full range visible. Use the typical estimate for a base route and the high or P80-like estimate for a commitment boundary when interruptions or uncertainty matter. Do not present the weighted estimate as a statistical guarantee.

Estimate separately:

- initial learning effort;
- practice and debugging effort;
- feedback or supervision wait;
- retrieval/spacing time;
- project integration time;
- setup and teardown;
- maintenance effort;
- contingency or recovery reserve.

## 4. Estimate by evidence, not topic count

Time depends on target proficiency and evidence context. Increase the range when the learner must work independently, transfer to unfamiliar problems, debug, compare alternatives, communicate decisions, or produce a durable artifact. Reduce only when diagnostic evidence justifies skipping work.

Do not estimate “learning Python” as one block. Estimate the observable capabilities, their practice, integration, and evidence. A faster reading pass is not equivalent to reliable performance.

## 5. Convert effort into calendar time

Use the learner’s availability windows, not an invented weekly timetable. A basic conversion is:

`calendar duration = effort and review demand ÷ reliable usable capacity`,

then add dependency waits, required spacing, fixed commitments, interruption risk, and recovery reserve. State which component controls the result: effort, spacing, dependency, feedback wait, or availability.

For recurring schedules, define:

- available windows and their reliability;
- preferred session length and maximum sustainable focus block;
- minimum and maximum spacing between sessions;
- protected buffer;
- no-study or recovery windows;
- rescheduling policy after a miss;
- weekly or cycle-level review point.

Do not backfill every missed session. Preserve recovery reserve and re-estimate the remaining work.

## 6. Model dependencies and the critical path

For each milestone, identify prerequisites, parallelizable work, shared artifacts, feedback waits, and the evidence gate. The portfolio’s calendar lower bound is controlled by the critical path and spacing constraints, not by the sum of every branch. The learner’s actual completion range is wider because parallel work consumes switching capacity.

Show at least three route views when useful:

- **Effort view:** total focused effort by phase and family;
- **Calendar view:** elapsed time under stated availability and spacing;
- **Critical-path view:** dependencies and waits that can delay the north star.

## 7. Capacity-aware allocation

Allocate time in this order:

1. required critical-path work;
2. minimum practice and evidence for the primary branch;
3. maintenance of completed capabilities;
4. recovery reserve;
5. one low-conflict parallel branch if capacity remains;
6. optional breadth and exploration.

Do not spend all available time on planned tasks. A sustainable plan normally leaves explicit reserve. If the learner requests an aggressive plan, show the increased risk: less recovery, more switching, weaker retention, or reduced evidence quality.

## 8. Track actuals and recalibrate

For each completed session or cycle, capture planned effort, actual effort, interruption, rework, evidence result, and reason for variance. Re-estimate remaining work using recent comparable actuals, preferably a median or conservative range. Separate systematic bias from one-off shocks.

Useful variance categories include under-scoped work, unfamiliar prerequisite, environment/setup friction, poor focus window, interruption, feedback delay, over-high evidence standard, and genuine faster completion. Change the estimate, decomposition, capacity assumption, or route—not all four at once without explanation.

Replan when the estimate error repeats, the remaining work changes materially, the learner’s availability changes, evidence fails, or a dependency/technology changes. Preserve completed work and stable IDs.

## 9. Report estimates responsibly

Every time-sensitive output should state:

- the unit being estimated;
- effort versus calendar time;
- low/typical/high range and confidence;
- assumptions about current skill and evidence standard;
- availability and reserve assumptions;
- the dominant uncertainty;
- the next calibration point;
- what would make the estimate invalid.

If the learner has not supplied enough information, provide a conditional estimate with explicit assumptions rather than inventing a precise date. A time estimate answers “what planning range is plausible under these conditions,” not “when mastery is guaranteed.”
